Depressive Disorders

Major depressive disorder, persistent depressive disorder, and related mood disorders
⚡ Quick Hits MDD = 5+ symptoms, 2+ weeks, must include depressed mood OR anhedonia · Persistent Depressive Disorder (dysthymia) = milder symptoms lasting 2+ years (1 yr children) · Serotonin syndrome = medical emergency (SHIVERS) · MAOI + tyramine foods = hypertensive crisis · Antidepressants have a Boxed Warning for increased suicidality in clients ≤24 · Full antidepressant effect takes 4–8 weeks even though some improvement can appear in 1–2 weeks
Depression Overview: Mood vs. Affect
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾

Mood = a pervasive and sustained emotion (the person's internal, longer-lasting emotional climate). Affect = the current, observable emotional state/expression — how mood is displayed outwardly.

  • Depression affects an estimated 8% of adults in the U.S. annually; ~280 million people worldwide.
  • Women are diagnosed with depression roughly twice as often as men (10–20% lifetime risk in women vs. 5–10% in men); risk becomes roughly 50/50 later in life.
  • Depression is predicted to become a leading cause of disability worldwide.
  • Types of mood: Elevated (can involve delusions/hallucinations), Euthymic (normal baseline), Dysphoric (chronic low mood ≥2 yrs, more days than not), Irritable (easily annoyed/provoked to anger).
RationaleA disorder of mood exists when intensity is beyond normal range, is not a normal response to life events, and interferes with daily functioning.
Types of Depressive Disorders
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾
  • Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) — a discrete episode of ≥2 weeks; can be single or recurrent; subtypes include melancholia, postpartum/peripartum, psychotic, agitated, and seasonal.
  • Persistent Depressive Disorder (Dysthymia) — chronic, milder depression present most of the day, more days than not, for ≥2 years in adults (≥1 year in children/adolescents, mood may be irritable). Often described as "down in the dumps"; may feel like part of one's personality.
  • Seasonal Affective Disorder (SAD) — MDD with seasonal pattern; ~5% of U.S. adults; occurs fall/winter, remits in spring; linked to reduced daylight and circadian shift; more common further from the equator; treated with light therapy (2,500–10,000 lux, 20+ min/day, mornings).
  • Peripartum/Postpartum Depression — occurs during pregnancy or up to 1 year after delivery. Distinguish from "baby blues."
  • Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD) — severe mood/physical symptoms in the final week before menses, minimal/absent after; ≥5 symptoms total (affective lability, irritability, depressed mood/hopelessness, anxiety + others).
  • Substance/Medication-Induced Depressive Disorder — depressive symptoms directly tied temporally to substance use, misuse, or withdrawal (alcohol, cannabis, steroids, antihypertensives, etc.).
  • Depressive Disorder Due to Another Medical Condition — caused by the physiological effects of a medical illness (stroke, Parkinson's, Cushing's, hypothyroidism, cancer, etc.).
  • Mixed Features — depressive episode with some (not predominant) manic/hypomanic symptoms; significant risk factor for later Bipolar I/II diagnosis.

DSM-5-TR Criteria for Major Depressive Disorder
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾

5 or more of the following present during the same 2-week period, representing a change from baseline; at least one must be (1) depressed mood or (2) loss of interest/pleasure:

  • Depressed mood most of the day, nearly every day (irritable mood in children/adolescents)
  • Markedly diminished interest or pleasure in almost all activities (anhedonia)
  • Significant weight loss/gain or appetite change (>5% body weight/month)
  • Insomnia or hypersomnia nearly every day
  • Psychomotor agitation or retardation (observable by others)
  • Fatigue or loss of energy
  • Feelings of worthlessness or excessive/inappropriate guilt
  • Diminished ability to think/concentrate, or indecisiveness
  • Recurrent thoughts of death, suicidal ideation (with/without plan), or suicide attempt

Must cause clinically significant distress/impairment; not attributable to a substance or medical condition; client has never had a manic or hypomanic episode.

Causes & Neurobiology of Depression
▾
  • Brain regions: Hippocampus (memory; smaller/impaired growth in depression from chronic stress hormones), Amygdala (emotion; hyperactive in depression, persists after recovery), Hypothalamus (stress response via HPA axis).
  • HPA axis: Hypothalamus → CRH → pituitary → ACTH → adrenal glands → cortisol. Chronically elevated CRH/cortisol is linked to depression; antidepressants and ECT reduce CRH levels as symptoms improve.
  • Neurotransmitters: Serotonin (mood, sleep, appetite, pain — low levels linked to suicide risk), Norepinephrine (anxiety, motivation/reward), Dopamine (motivation, reality perception, reward — dysregulation linked to anhedonia and psychosis), Glutamate (excitatory; role in bipolar/schizophrenia), GABA (inhibitory, subdues anxiety; low levels linked to MDD), Acetylcholine (memory/learning; increased levels linked to depression).
  • Genetics: Mood disorders run in families; dozens of genes involved; genetic vulnerability + stress can push a person's mood system off balance.
  • Sociocultural factors: Stressful life events, relationship/marital stress, socioeconomic instability, gender inequality, and Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs — abuse, neglect, household dysfunction) — ~61% of adults report ≥1 ACE; toxic stress from ACEs alters brain development and stress response.
  • Medical conditions account for ~10–15% of all depressions: hypothyroidism, heart disease, stroke, MS/Parkinson's/Alzheimer's, B12 deficiency, endocrine disorders, HIV/hepatitis, cancer, chronic pain.
  • Medications/substances that can cause depressive symptoms: steroids, some BP medications, corticosteroids, antiepileptics, oral contraceptives, barbiturates, alcohol, cannabis.
📋 Prof. NoteAntidepressants raise neurotransmitter levels immediately, but mood doesn't improve for weeks — this delay is explained by neurogenesis (new nerve cell growth/connections in the hippocampus), not just neurotransmitter levels.
Risk Factors for Depression (Box 9-2)
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾
  • Previous depressive episode
  • Female gender
  • Family history of depression
  • Stressful life events
  • Substance abuse or dependency
  • Postpartum period
  • History of suicide attempt
  • Chronic general medical condition

Average age of onset is the mid-20s; first episode is usually preceded by a major life stressor. Course can be a single episode or lifelong recurring episodes; some experience a steady, untreated depressed state lasting years. Presence of psychotic symptoms with depression is more disabling and needs more intensive treatment.

Mental Status Exam Findings in Depression
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾
  • Appearance/behavior: Disheveled, poor hygiene/body odor, weight loss/gain, constipation, vegetative signs, avolition ("I just don't want to shower"), anergia ("I have no energy"), social isolation, possible aggressiveness/agitation.
  • Sleep: Insomnia or hypersomnia (14–18 hrs); psychomotor retardation (slowed talk/walk/reaction) OR psychomotor agitation/restlessness.
  • Speech: Slow, monotone, delayed responses (latency).
  • Mood/Affect: Apathy, anxious/irritable/angry/tearful, labile mood, dysphoria, sadness, flat/blunted/constricted affect, incongruency between words and body language.
  • Thought/perception: Poverty of content, decreased attention span, obsessions/ruminations, psychotic depression can impair reality testing.
🔴 Critical — Notify Provider ImmediatelyHopelessness, worthlessness, and helplessness are directly linked to increased risk of self-injury/suicide. Suicidal, homicidal, or violence ideation must always be reported. Do not leave the client alone if these statements are being made.
Suicide Risk & Behavioral Clues of Impending Suicide
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾
  • ~700,000+ people die by suicide annually worldwide (~85 deaths/day cited in lecture).
  • Increased rate among adolescents/young adults; firearm-related suicide and firearms in the home are major contributing factors.
  • Suicide rates are highest among older adults, with white males at highest risk (rate ~6x higher); many had seen a primary care provider within 3 weeks prior to suicide.
  • The worst time of day for a suicidal person is often the morning.

Behavioral Clues of Impending Suicide

  • Any sudden change in behavior
  • Becomes energetic after a period of severe depression (⚠ high risk — energy to act on a plan)
  • Improved mood 10–14 days after starting an antidepressant may indicate suicidal plans have been made
  • Finalizes business/personal affairs; gives away valuable possessions or pets
  • Withdraws from social activities and plans; appears emotionally upset
  • Presence of weapons, razors, pills (means); has a specific death plan; leaves a note
  • Makes direct or indirect statements ("I may not be around then")
SafetyAlways directly ask about self-harm/suicidal ideation. Asking does NOT increase risk — it opens a vital safety dialogue and often provides relief. Remove dangerous items (pills, sharp objects, cords/belts/neckties/pantyhose). Institute 1:1 observation as indicated. Note: mortality ratio for suicide in bipolar disorder is ~20x the general population rate.
Grief vs. Depression vs. Prolonged Grief Disorder
▾
  • Grief: painful feelings come in waves, mixed with positive memories; self-esteem usually maintained; thoughts of death may relate to "joining" the deceased.
  • Major Depression: mood/interest decreased most of 2+ weeks; feelings of worthlessness/self-loathing common; thoughts focus on ending one's life due to feeling worthless or unable to cope.
  • Prolonged Grief Disorder (new DSM-5-TR diagnosis): intense longing/preoccupation with the deceased persisting >12 months with significant impairment, plus 3+ of: identity disruption, disbelief about the death, avoidance of reminders, intense emotional pain, difficulty reintegrating into relationships, emotional numbness, feeling life is meaningless, intense loneliness.
Treatments for Depression: Overview
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾

80–90% of people with depression respond well to treatment. Gold standard = medication + cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT).

  • Antidepressant medication classes: SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs, MAOIs, NDRI (bupropion), Novel/atypical agents (mirtazapine, vilazodone, vortioxetine, trazodone). See Drug Cards tab for full detail.
  • Psychotherapy: Cognitive therapy, Behavior therapy (CBT, DBT, hypnotherapy, art therapy), Interpersonal psychotherapy (12–16 structured sessions focused on role transitions, grief, interpersonal disputes, social isolation), Exercise.
  • ECT — for severe depression not responding to medication/therapy.
  • Light therapy — for seasonal affective disorder.
  • Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS) — noninvasive, magnetic fields targeted at mood-regulating brain areas.

With treatment, ~80% of individuals with MDD resume normal activities within a few weeks. Antidepressants generally take 4–8 weeks for full effect, though some improvement can occur in 1–2 weeks (this early-improvement window is also a high suicide-risk period — see Suicide Risk card).

Family EducationFamilies may feel responsible or ask "what does he have to be depressed about?" — depression is a biological + environmental illness, not a character flaw, and education can reduce blame/shame.
Serotonin Syndrome
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾

A medical emergency caused by excess serotonin — from high antidepressant doses, combining serotonergic drugs (e.g., SSRI + MAOI, SSRI + St. John's Wort, SSRI + triptans). Onset often within 24 hours of the dose/combination change.

Mnemonic: SHIVERS Shivering · Hyperreflexia (+ myoclonus) · Increased temperature · Vital sign abnormalities (tachycardia, tachypnea, labile BP) · Encephalopathy (agitation, delirium, confusion) · Restlessness · Sweating

Categories: Mental status changes (agitation, delirium), Autonomic hyperactivity (tachycardia, HTN, hyperthermia, diaphoresis, vomiting/diarrhea), Neuromuscular hyperactivity (tremor, rigidity, myoclonus, hyperreflexia, clonus).

Treatment: Stop all serotonergic medications; supportive care (IV fluids, cooling, HR/BP control); benzodiazepines (diazepam/lorazepam) for agitation/rigidity; cyproheptadine to block serotonin production if symptoms persist; severe cases need sedation/intubation/paralysis/active cooling.

🔴 DangerUntreated serotonin syndrome can be fatal; uncontrolled muscle spasms can cause rhabdomyolysis → kidney damage. Can resemble Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS), but NMS develops over days-to-weeks vs. serotonin syndrome's rapid (within 24h) onset.
MAOI Hypertensive Crisis & Tyramine
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾

MAOIs inhibit the breakdown of tyramine. Eating tyramine-rich foods, or combining with pseudoephedrine, causes a hypertensive crisis: BP >180/120 with acute end-organ damage risk (stroke, MI, kidney damage).

Symptoms: Severe headache, confusion, blurred vision, tachycardia or bradycardia, chest pain, neck stiffness, nausea/vomiting, diaphoresis, dilated pupils, photophobia, SOB, severe anxiety, seizures, intracranial bleeding possible.

Foods to Avoid with MAOIsAged cheese, cured/aged meats, liver, yogurt, herring, yeast, beer, red wine, sour cream, pickled products, fermented foods, overripe fruits (banana, avocado, fava beans), caffeine, antihistamines, amphetamines/pseudoephedrine.

Treatment: Discontinue offending agent; IV antihypertensives (phentolamine, labetalol); supportive/ICU care as needed.

Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT)
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾

Reserved for severe depression not responsive to medication/therapy; also used with catatonia or when rapid response is needed (e.g., high suicide risk). Given 2–3x/week for a total of 6–12 treatments, under general anesthesia with a muscle relaxant.

  • Pre-procedure: Written informed consent required; NPO after midnight (sip of water OK with AM meds); void before procedure; remove dentures/glasses/jewelry; atropine ~30 min before to decrease secretions; have O2 and suction available.
  • Procedure: Brief electrical stimulation causes a ~1-minute seizure; client asleep throughout; wakes in 5–10 minutes.
  • Post-procedure: Monitor vital signs, orient client, stay with client, watch for fall risk, observe for sudden mood improvement AND for indications of suicidal threats after treatment.
Side EffectsShort-term memory loss and difficulty learning are common; usually improves within a couple months, though some clients have longer-lasting gaps.
Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS)
▾

Noninvasive; uses magnetic fields (electromagnetic coil against forehead) to stimulate nerve cells in mood-regulating brain regions. Used when other treatments haven't worked. Unlike ECT, stimulation is targeted, not generalized; no anesthesia required. Session lasts 30–60 min. Electrical currents ~0.2–0.8 to cause a localized effect; opens new neuron pathways. Typically 6–12 treatments needed.

Side effects: knocking/tapping sensation, scalp/jaw/face muscle twitching, mild headache, brief light-headedness; seizure is a rare possible adverse effect.

Depression in Older Adults & Geriatric Depression Scale
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾
  • Different presentation: vague physical complaints/somatization, unlikely to volunteer "I'm depressed" to the physician; may say "I don't want to be a burden."
  • Diagnosis complicated by cognitive impairment; symptoms may be dismissed as "normal aging."
  • 1.5–3x greater risk of death if depressed (may stop eating, refuse treatments like dialysis).
  • Untreated depression can worsen memory/thinking problems even in existing dementia.

Geriatric Depression Scale (Short Form, Box 9-6): 15 yes/no questions (e.g., "Do you feel your life is empty?" "Do you often feel helpless?"). Scoring: count "bold"/indicative responses — a score of 5+ indicates need for referral; a score of 10 is almost always associated with depression.

Stigma of Depression & Mental Illness
▾
  • Requirement to report mental illness can affect employment/licensure concerns
  • Medical insurance coverage may be lower for mental health, or denied if treatment adherence is questioned
  • Physicians may avoid entering the diagnosis to protect clients from stigma
  • ~80% of depression treatment occurs outside a mental health setting
  • Fear of stigma delays or prevents people from seeking treatment

Bipolar Disorders

Bipolar I, Bipolar II, cyclothymia, mania, and mood-stabilizing treatment
⚡ Quick Hits Bipolar I = at least ONE full manic episode (hospitalization-level) · Bipolar II = hypomania + depression, never full mania · Cyclothymia = 2+ yrs of hypomanic + depressive symptoms not meeting full criteria · Manic episode = 1+ week, 3+ symptoms (4 if only irritable) · Lithium therapeutic range 0.6–1.2 mEq/L (some sources 0.8–1.2); levels >2.5 mEq/L = medical emergency · Avoid NSAIDs with lithium (increases levels) · Estrogen may be protective against psychosis-spectrum illness
Bipolar Disorder Overview
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾

A mood disorder causing dramatic shifts between manic (abnormally elevated) episodes and depressive (abnormal low) episodes. Reality contact is less disturbed than in schizophrenia, although severe mania can include psychotic features (hallucinations/delusions), which can be mistaken for schizophrenia.

  • Three major types: Bipolar I, Bipolar II, Cyclothymic Disorder.
  • Elation/grandiosity can be a defense against underlying low self-esteem/depression.
  • Testing/manipulative behavior often stems from poor self-esteem.
  • Common coexisting disorders: anxiety, ADHD, substance use disorders, eating disorders.

🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾

DSM-5-TR Criteria: Manic & Hypomanic Episodes
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾

Manic episode: Persistently elevated or irritable mood + abnormally increased energy, lasting ≥1 week (any duration if hospitalization required), causing marked impairment. 3+ (or 4 if mood is only irritable) of:

  • Inflated self-esteem or grandiosity
  • Decreased need for sleep (feels rested after only 3 hrs)
  • More talkative than usual / pressured speech
  • Flight of ideas / racing thoughts
  • Distractibility
  • Increase in goal-directed activity or psychomotor agitation
  • Excessive involvement in high-risk activities (spending sprees, sexual indiscretion, risky investments)

Hypomanic episode: Same symptom list, but lasts ≥4 consecutive days and does NOT cause marked impairment or require hospitalization. If psychotic features are present, it is by definition manic, not hypomanic.

Assessment Findings During a Manic Episode
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾
  • Constant/indiscriminate enthusiasm, fluctuating between elation and irritability
  • Activity feels productive to the client but is actually disorganized and unproductive
  • Grandiose delusions, decreased need for sleep
  • Rapid/pressured speech, easily distractible, multi-tasker with poor outcomes
  • Unwarranted optimism, grandiosity, poor judgment — impairs functioning and can threaten lives
  • Disoriented/incoherent possible but can be oriented x3; euphoria or angry/hostile mood; flight of ideas; impulsive, eccentric; inappropriate dress/excessive makeup/jewelry; loud rapid talking; lacks inhibition/social blunders; sarcastic/profane/abusive language; quick-tempered, agitated; talks excessively, jokes/dances/sings/hyperactive; can't stop moving to eat; weight loss/decreased appetite; insomnia; regressed behavior; sexually indiscreet/hypersexual
  • Extended sleep deprivation during mania can trigger hallucinations
Clinical NoteManic patients may hit on the nurse, invade personal space, believe they are the most important person on the unit. Nursing response: maintain professional boundaries, redirect calmly, do not laugh at jokes or reinforce performing.
Depressive Episodes Within Bipolar Disorder
▾

Same DSM-5-TR criteria as MDD (5+ symptoms, 2 weeks). Depressive episodes are often severe/debilitating and carry high suicide risk — mortality ratio for suicide in bipolar disorder is ~20x the general population.

  • Weight gain or loss, anorexia; insomnia or hypersomnia; decreased sexual desire; fatigue, decreased activity
  • May feel hopeless, unhappy, helpless, miserable, guilty
  • Regressive thinking: passive, dependent, decreased motivation, concerned with bodily functions, impaired memory, distorted thought content, slow/diminished speech
  • Delusions possible; socially withdrawn; poor self-esteem
Causes of Bipolar Disorder
▾
  • Genetic component: strong hereditary link; multigene inheritance disorder; higher risk if parent/sibling affected
  • Biochemical: norepinephrine and dopamine implicated; altered intracellular calcium signaling seen in newer research (why antiseizure/anticonvulsant meds are effective mood stabilizers)
  • Average age of onset: early 20s; children may show behavior problems before the first episode; episodes begin suddenly
  • Psychosocial factors influence the timing of episodes (biological condition, but stress can trigger an episode)
  • Without treatment, episodes average ~4/year; episodes can last weeks to months; psychosis with an episode = more serious, higher risk of aggression/suicide
  • Often involvement with alcohol/drugs (self-medication)
Lithium — Gold Standard Mood Stabilizer
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾

Naturally occurring element, chemically similar to sodium. Reduces manic symptoms within 1–3 weeks and has unique antisuicidal properties.

  • Therapeutic range: 0.6–1.2 mEq/L (initial/acute target sometimes cited as 1–1.5 mEq/L, maintenance 0.8–1.2 mEq/L). Levels >2.5 mEq/L = medical emergency even if asymptomatic.
  • Compliance is a major issue — side effects may feel intolerable; client education is essential.
  • Common side effects: nausea, dry mouth, diarrhea, thirst, drowsiness, mild hand tremor, polyuria, weight gain, bloated feeling — polyuria/polydipsia in ~60% of clients, usually improve after 3–6 weeks.
  • Contraindicated in cardiovascular disease, renal disease, decreased sodium intake/severe dehydration, and with diuretics (all increase toxicity risk); NSAIDs also increase lithium levels — avoid.
  • Long-term use risk: hypothyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, impaired kidney function, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, neuropathy.
  • Blood levels drawn 10–12 hrs after last dose (trough, AM prior to AM dose); monitor 2–3x/week initially, then monthly on maintenance.
  • Take with food to reduce GI upset; maintain consistent sodium intake and 1.5–3 L/day fluid intake.

Anticonvulsant Mood Stabilizers (Valproate, Carbamazepine, Lamotrigine)
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾
  • Valproic Acid / Valproate (Depakote) — first-line drug, used alone or combined with lithium; approved for mania since 1995; used for rapid cycling not responding to other stabilizers. Works by increasing GABA inhibition and suppressing sodium/calcium influx into neurons. Rapid onset. Therapeutic range 50–125 mcg/mL. Side effects: weight gain, sedation, nausea/vomiting, hair loss, tremors, GI upset, menstrual disturbances. Requires LFTs, amylase/lipase, platelet monitoring (risk of blood dyscrasias, pancreatitis, hepatotoxicity). Contraindicated in pregnancy — reduced fetal IQ risk.
  • Carbamazepine (Tegretol) — used for clients not responding to lithium, especially rapid-cycling, paranoia, extreme hyperactivity. Requires routine WBC + LFT monitoring (bone marrow suppression, liver damage risk — most serious adverse effect is agranulocytosis, get a weekly CBC when starting). Therapeutic range ~4–12 mcg/mL; toxicity risk if >20 mcg/mL. Side effects: hyponatremia, fatigue, blurred vision, nausea, ataxia. Avoid combining with SSRIs (both affect cytochrome P450).
  • Lamotrigine (Lamictal) — used for acute mania or maintenance, including bipolar depression; works on GABA system, inhibits neuronal firing. Therapeutic range 2.5–15 mcg/mL. Boxed warning: Stevens-Johnson Syndrome — potentially fatal skin reaction (moderate rash risk 1–2% in children) — flu-like symptoms followed by painful, blistering, spreading rash; skin becomes necrotic; reversible if medication stopped promptly.
Nursing Management of Mania/Hyperactivity
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾
  • Decrease stimuli: single room away from activity, keep noise low, soft lighting, limit people, remove client to a quiet area, avoid competitive activities/games
  • Distract and redirect energy: brief-attention-span activities (chores, walks), large-movement physical activities (dance) until acute mania subsides, writing materials for "busy work" once mania eases
  • External controls: assign one consistent staff person; don't laugh/encourage jokes or performing; accompany client to room when escalating; guard against suicide as mood evens out (elation subsiding into depression is a high-risk window)
  • Manage manipulativeness: set firm limits (phone calls, meeting times); refuse unreasonable demands; explain restrictions and reasons; be consistent across staff; use short, clear sentences; avoid long discussions/giving advice; never threaten or compare to other clients
  • Physical needs: offer fluids every hour (won't stop to drink); high-calorie finger foods carried while moving; serve meals in room if too stimulated; encourage rest, PRN sedation, short naps; supervise bathing (may play in water or be too distracted to clean self)
  • Decrease denial: reflect feelings, function as a role model for open communication, help client recognize demanding behavior and needs of others, encourage direct verbalization of needs
  • Store client valuables in the hospital safe until judgment returns
Psychotherapy & Lifestyle Management for Bipolar Disorder
▾
  • CBT, DBT, psychoeducation
  • Interpersonal and Social Rhythm Therapy (IPSRT) — emphasizes stable daily routines (sleep/wake, work, meals)
  • Family-focused therapy — psychoeducation, communication training, problem-solving, family dynamics
  • ECT can be used for severe manic/depressive episodes unresponsive to meds, or when rapid response needed (suicide risk, catatonia)
  • Regular aerobic exercise, Mediterranean/balanced diet, regular sleep schedule, mindfulness, avoiding alcohol/tobacco/recreational drugs, strong social connections
  • Keeping a life/mood chart to track symptoms, treatments, sleep, life events over time
🟢 Clinical PearlPriorities in collaborative care are always safety first, then treating the mood disorder — with a comprehensive approach including education, psychotherapy, and active client involvement in recovery.

Schizophrenia & Psychosis

Psychosis, delirium, schizophrenia symptoms, subtypes, and antipsychotic treatment
⚡ Quick Hits Schizophrenia = 2+ symptoms for a significant portion of ≥1 month, continuous signs ≥6 months, functional decline · Positive symptoms = ADDED (hallucinations, delusions, disorganized speech) — respond well to antipsychotics · Negative symptoms = LOST (flat affect, alogia, avolition, anhedonia) — worsen over time, less med-responsive · Onset 14–24 (males earlier/more severe; females later, ~25–35, more positive sx, estrogen protective) · NMS = life-threatening; hold antipsychotic + dantrolene/bromocriptine · DSM-5 no longer uses subtypes but you'll still see them charted clinically
Psychosis: Definition & Psychotic Episode
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾

Psychosis = a broad symptom complex of hallucinations, delusions, and disorganized thinking; can occur in schizophrenia, mood disorders, substance-induced states, or medical conditions. ~3% of people in the U.S. experience psychosis at some point; ~100,000 adolescents/young adults have a first episode yearly.

Brief psychotic episode (DSM-5-TR): delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, or grossly disorganized/catatonic behavior lasting ≥1 day but <1 month, with eventual return to baseline functioning.

Early warning signs: drop in grades/job performance, new trouble concentrating, suspiciousness/paranoia, social withdrawal, unusual/bizarre new ideas, strange or absent feelings, decline in self-care, confused speech.

Delirium vs. Psychosis vs. Dementia
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾

Delirium = a temporary, sudden-onset state of confusion/disorientation, usually caused by an underlying medical issue — it resolves once the cause is treated.

Common causes: dehydration/electrolyte imbalance, dementia, ICU hospitalization, alcohol/drug intoxication or withdrawal, kidney/liver failure, medications (sedatives, opioids, anesthesia, antihistamines, anticholinergics, antidepressants, antipsychotics, anticonvulsants), DKA/metabolic disorders, serious infection (UTI, pneumonia, influenza), severe pain, sleep deprivation. Advanced age = higher vulnerability.

Symptoms (onset over hours-days, fluctuating): changes in alertness, changing consciousness levels, confusion, disorganized thinking/speech, disrupted sleep, emotional changes, hallucinations/delusions, incontinence, short-term memory problems, trouble concentrating.

Management: quiet/well-lit room, clocks/calendars visible, family presence, hearing aids/glasses worn, undisrupted sleep, get client up/moving when possible, encourage calories/hydration, control pain, low-dose antipsychotic (e.g., haloperidol) if distressed/at risk — use cautiously as oversedation worsens delirium; avoid restraints.

Schizophrenia Overview
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾
  • Complex brain disorder affecting thinking, feeling, and behavior; alters perception, thought, feelings, behaviors.
  • Occurs in ~1 out of 100 people (1% of the population).
  • Includes "lack of insight"/anosognosia — inability to recognize one's own illness.
  • Continuous signs must be present ≥6 months, with medical causes of delirium ruled out.
  • Typically diagnosed late teens–early 30s; emerges earlier in males than females.
  • Often clients experience considerable distress in personal/work life, are underrepresented in research funding and services, and suffer prejudice/discrimination.
  • Only ~60% of individuals with schizophrenia receive treatment; more are found in jail (~40%) than hospitals; often homeless and victims of crime.
DSM-5-TR Diagnostic Criteria for Schizophrenia
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾

2+ of the following present for a significant portion of a 1-month period (or less if treated); at least ONE must be delusions, hallucinations, or disorganized speech:

  • Delusions
  • Hallucinations
  • Disorganized speech (derailment/incoherence)
  • Grossly disorganized or catatonic behavior
  • Negative symptoms (diminished emotional expression or avolition)

Plus: significant decline in functioning (work, relationships, self-care) from prior baseline; continuous signs persist ≥6 months (may include prodromal/residual periods with only negative symptoms); depressive/bipolar disorders with psychotic features and substance/medical causes must be ruled out.

Positive Symptoms (Psychosis)
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾

"Positive" = symptoms ADDED to normal functioning; caused by high dopamine in the brain; respond well to antipsychotics. Drugs that increase dopamine (methamphetamines, amphetamines, levodopa) can also trigger these symptoms.

  • Hallucinations — false sensory perceptions without external stimulus; client may/may not recognize they aren't real. Auditory most common (heard as voices), then visual; can also be tactile, olfactory, or somatic.
  • Delusions — fixed, false beliefs not shared by cultural peers, held despite contradictory evidence. Types: Persecutory (someone is after them), Referential (TV/radio talking to them), Somatic (body has a terrible illness), Religious (they are God/possessed), Grandiose (inflated importance), Ideas of reference (trivial events feel directed at self).
  • Disorganized speech/thinking — inability to sort/interpret information; incoherent speech; loose associations (disconnected ideas); can progress to word salad.
Nursing TipDo not argue with the client about a delusion's reality — acknowledge the feeling behind it, redirect gently: "I don't hear that, but it sounds frightening to you."
Negative Symptoms
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾

"Negative" = normal functions LOST/diminished; less responsive to antipsychotic medication than positive symptoms; tend to worsen with time.

4 Key Negative Symptoms Flat affect — reduced expression of emotion via face/voice · Alogia — poverty of speech (lack of speech) · Avolition — reduced motivation/goal-directed behavior (lack of motivation) · Anhedonia — diminished pleasure in daily life (no joy)

Also includes: social withdrawal, self-neglect. Social withdrawal is sometimes an early warning sign of an approaching psychotic episode.

Disorganized Symptoms & Cognitive Symptoms
▾
  • Disorganized behavior: lacks goal orientation (due to lack of motivation), ADLs difficult, unusual/purposeless behavior, odd dress, altered sense of self affects behavior/movement, inappropriate personal behavior (often starts before diagnosis), possible catatonia.
  • Cognitive symptoms: trouble focusing/attention, difficulty processing information to make decisions, problems using newly learned information, memory/executive functioning/reasoning deficits.
Clinical NoteAn example of incongruent affect: a client reports their dog died and they were close to it, but laughs while describing this — flat or incongruent affect is a hallmark of disorganized/negative symptomatology.

Causes & Risk Factors for Schizophrenia
▾
  • Genetics: tends to run in families; many genes involved, no single gene causes it; identical twins — if one has schizophrenia, the other has ~55% concordance; offspring of an affected parent ~16% risk (lecture cited up to 60% risk if both parents affected).
  • Environment: Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs), prenatal viral exposure/nutritional problems, urban upbringing, migration, early-life adversity. Cannabis use worsens psychosis symptoms in genetically predisposed individuals and increases relapse/hospitalization.
  • Brain structure/function: differences in brain region volumes, connectivity, and neurotransmitters (dopamine); increased ventricle size and decreased cerebral blood flow seen on autopsy/CT/MRI; changes may begin before birth; puberty-related brain changes can trigger psychosis in vulnerable individuals.
  • Resilience is a protective factor — a genetically predisposed child in a stable, supportive home may never develop the disorder; genetic predisposition alone does not guarantee illness.
Course of Disease, Occurrence & Gender Differences
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾
  • Onset typically 14–24 years old (young adulthood), though can occur at any age; onset before age 13 is rare.
  • Positive symptoms tend to plateau ~5–10 years after diagnosis; negative symptoms worsen over time.
  • Most clients are unmarried, more likely unemployed; some do live full, meaningful lives.
  • Course includes exacerbations (psychotic breaks) and prodromal phases, similar to a remitting/relapsing disease pattern.
  • Men: earlier onset (15–25), more severe course. Women: later onset (25–35), more positive symptoms, higher medication compliance, longer medication half-life; estrogen appears protective against schizophrenia and may explain lower prevalence/later onset in women.
  • Individuals with schizophrenia die at earlier ages (suicide, injuries, disease, homelessness); can develop dementia at younger ages than the general population; may not recognize they are mentally ill (anosognosia).
🟢 Clinical PearlEarly diagnosis and treatment (ideally at the very first psychotic episode) may prevent the worst long-term outcomes — this is the basis for Coordinated Specialty Care programs. Dual diagnosis (mental illness + substance use) is common.
Treatment & Management Overview
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾
  • Psychopharmacology is the cornerstone of treatment — antipsychotics relieve hallucinations, delusions, disordered thinking.
  • May take several medication trials to find what works; full effect takes 3–6 weeks.
  • Medication compliance is a major barrier: 80% who stop meds relapse within a year; even with compliance, ~30% still experience a relapse.
  • Psychosocial treatments: CBT, behavioral skills training, supported employment, cognitive remediation — reduce relapse/hospitalization risk.
  • Family education and support reduces caregiver distress and improves outcomes.
  • Coordinated Specialty Care (CSC) — team-based recovery-oriented care for first-episode psychosis (psychotherapy, medication management, case management, employment/education support, family support); more effective than typical care.
  • Assertive Community Treatment (ACT) — multidisciplinary team for clients at risk of repeated hospitalization/homelessness; reduces both.
  • Milieu therapy: using the therapeutic environment itself — client responsible for own behavior, consistent staff as role models, practice/feedback on behavior, common goal of reacting to stress/frustration appropriately, peer pressure can encourage change.
Antipsychotic Side Effects: EPS, TD, NMS
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾

Extrapyramidal Side Effects (EPS)

  • Dystonia — involuntary muscle contractions (face, neck, tongue, larynx); laryngeal dystonia is a medical emergency (can need intubation); treat with parenteral anticholinergics (diphenhydramine, benztropine) — resolves in 10–30 min
  • Pseudoparkinsonism — slowed movement, muscle stiffness/rigidity, shuffling gait, excessive salivation, tremor, mask-like face
  • Akathisia — restlessness, inability to sit still (motor restlessness)
  • Tardive Dyskinesia (TD) — uncontrollable movements (lip smacking/puckering, eye blinking, grimacing, tongue thrusting, chewing motions); can persist for months–years even after stopping the drug; primary treatment = discontinue first-gen antipsychotic; VMAT2 inhibitors (deutetrabenazine, valbenazine) are first-line TD treatment

Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS)

Rare but potentially fatal; caused by dopamine blockade; develops over days-to-weeks (vs. rapid onset of serotonin syndrome).

  • Signs: temperature >100.4°F, severe muscle rigidity ("lead-pipe rigidity"), confusion/altered LOC, agitation/delirium up to coma, hyperreflexia, diaphoresis, elevated WBC/CPK/liver enzymes, myoglobinuria, autonomic instability (unstable BP), acute renal failure risk.
  • Treatment: stop the antipsychotic immediately; dantrolene and bromocriptine typically prescribed; hydration, cooling, close vital sign/electrolyte monitoring.

Other Adverse Effects

  • Anticholinergic (dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, urinary retention), sedation, weight gain, orthostatic hypotension, seizures, photosensitivity, metabolic syndrome, endocrine effects.
  • Diabetes — clients on antipsychotics are ~9% more likely to develop it.
🔴 SafetyNMS and Serotonin Syndrome can look similar — NMS develops over days-weeks vs. serotonin syndrome's rapid onset within 24 hours. Both require stopping the causative drug and supportive care.

Nursing Assessment: Hallucinations & Delusions
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾

Hallucination assessment questions: Which senses are involved? How long — when did it first start? Has it changed over time? What situations/times trigger it? What are the hallucinations about (theme)? Explore reality of the hallucination. Are they command hallucinations (telling the client to hurt self/others)? How does the client feel about it? What coping strategies do they use?

Assessing delusions: Determine if the client is able to reality-test (question their own thoughts). Ask if they believe there is any danger related to the delusion.

Wording MattersAsk "What do you hear?" not "What are the voices saying?" — avoid implying the perception is real, but also avoid arguing it isn't.

Nursing diagnoses: Risk for Violence, Disturbed Thought Processes, Sensory Perceptual Alterations, Ineffective Coping, Self-Care Deficit, Impaired Social Interaction, Impaired Communication, Imbalanced Nutrition, Sleep Deprivation, Social Isolation, Hopelessness, Spiritual Distress.

Risk for Violence: Interventions
🔥 HIGH YIELD
▾
  • Avoid touching the client during active hallucinations
  • Early intervention at first signs of agitation (increased motor activity, pacing, or a sudden stop — "calm before the storm")
  • Avoid confronting aggressively; use nonthreatening body language (arms to side, palms outward, keep distance, avoid blocking exits/body contact); identify yourself, speak calmly/firmly
  • Use the least restrictive interventions possible; maintain a low-stimulation environment
  • Talk with the client about signs/symptoms/triggers of their anxiety/agitation (a shared "code word" can help them signal escalating distress)
  • Observe closely during paranoid ideation or command hallucinations
  • If restraint is needed, get help (at least 4 people), move in an organized/calm manner, state you want to help and won't permit harm to self/others, use restraints correctly (never tie to a bed rail)

Lecture Transcript — Mood Disorders (Depression & Bipolar)

Mood is defined as a person's temporary state of mind exhibited through feelings and emotions. Experiencing different types and levels of moods is normal. Mood is considered abnormal when a person has problems with daily functioning because of exaggerated feelings and emotions.

The DSM-5 defines a mood disorder as one in which the predominant feature is a disturbance in mood — a range from extremely low (depression) to extremely high or irritable (manic). These fluctuations are believed to be caused from a biological basis that decreases the amount of norepinephrine and serotonin in the brain.

Three types of mood disorders: Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), Bipolar I disorder, Bipolar II disorder.

Statistics: 22% of U.S. adults have reported symptoms of depression. Women are diagnosed with depression twice as much as men; women have a 10–20% lifetime risk vs. men having a 5–10% lifetime risk; in later life the risk between genders is 50/50. Depression is the most prevalent mental health problem and is predicted to become the leading cause of disability.

Mood, Affect & Types of Mood

  • Mood — pervasive and sustained emotion.
  • Affect — current emotional state; affect can act on/produce a change in a person.
  • Alterations of affect primarily associated with depression: low self-esteem, feelings of anxiousness and dread, feelings of worthlessness ("I knew it was going to be a bad day" — referred to as being like "Eeyore").
  • Elevated mood — can cause alterations in perceptions, delusions, or hallucinations (patients may hear voices saying "you are no good and don't deserve your family").
  • Euthymic mood — normal, homeostatic mood state.
  • Dysphoric mood — chronic disturbance involving depressed mood for at least two years, more days than not.
  • Irritable mood — person is easily annoyed and provoked to anger.

Disorders of mood occur when mood is beyond the normal range of intensity, is not a normal response to life experiences, and interferes with daily functioning.

Objective Signs of Depression

  • Alterations in activity — psychomotor agitation or retardation, decline in ADLs, eating more or less than required, apathy.
  • Alterations in social interactions — poor social skills, underachieving, withdrawal from society.

MDD, Dysthymic Disorder, Bipolar I & II

MDD is diagnosed when one or more depressive episodes — defined as at least two weeks of depressed mood or loss of interest accompanied by at least four additional symptoms — occur. MDD can be a single or recurrent episode.

Dysthymic (persistent depressive) disorder is diagnosed when at least two years of depressed mood, for 50% of the time, is experienced. Does not meet criteria for MDD but is a chronic disorder. Symptoms: depressed mood most of the day, poor appetite or overeating, insomnia or hypersomnia, low energy/fatigue, low self-esteem, poor concentration, feelings of hopelessness.

Bipolar I and II are diagnosed with an understanding of manic episodes, hypomanic episodes, and major depression. Disorders divided into: Bipolar I, Bipolar II, Cyclothymic Disorder, and Substance/Medication-Induced Bipolar Disorder. Cyclothymic disorder is diagnosed by a swing between hypomanic episodes and dysphoric episodes.

Depression is the oldest and most frequently described psychiatric illness — has existed since biblical times (King Saul, Job, Elijah, Jeremiah). Notable historical figures who suffered depression: Ernest Hemingway, Abraham Lincoln, Winston Churchill, Brooke Shields; Britney Spears has bipolar disorder.

MDD is a disorder of severity but is treatable — with treatment, 80% of individuals resume normal activities within a few weeks. Families may feel responsible for the loved one and want a medical diagnosis due to shame/guilt, or may wonder what the patient has to feel depressed about ("nice house, great kids, great job") — reflecting a lack of understanding of the illness.

Biological Theories

Neurochemical changes alter neurotransmitter levels causing depression — most commonly implicated are norepinephrine, serotonin, and dopamine; also acetylcholine and GABA. Serotonin plus stress increases risk of depression; medications such as SSRIs/SNRIs help regulate these imbalances.

Genetic theories: depression can be inherited through predisposition. Twin studies suggest a two-thirds risk of developing MDD if both parents have this diagnosis. Depression is 1.5–3x more common among first-degree relatives of affected people. Mothers who are depressed tend to raise children who become depressed.

Endocrine theories: disruption/malfunction of the HPA (hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal) axis, which regulates stress response, leads to cortisol, thyroid, and hormonal abnormalities. Over time this dysregulation causes hypersensitivity to stressors and increases depression risk.

60–90% of major depression patients suffer from sleep disturbances. Circadian rhythm changes due to medications, nutritional status, physical/psychosocial illness, hormonal imbalances, aging, night shift work increase depression/MDD risk.

Brain anatomy: depression is a result of or cause of changes in brain anatomy, such as atrophy of specific areas. Leading hypothesis: chronic depression is linked to reduction in serum brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), affecting plasticity of neurons. Deficiency of BDNF leads to apoptosis of proteins and atrophy of specific brain areas — the hippocampus is most affected. PET scans show abnormal function in prefrontal cortex/cerebrum and limbic systems during episodes.

Risk Factors for Depression

Previous depressive episodes, female gender, family history, stressful life events, substance abuse/dependency, postpartum periods, history of suicide attempt, chronic general medical conditions. Important to ask patients about history/past to link back these risk factors.

Depression can occur at any age, average age of onset is mid-20s. First episode usually preceded by a major life stressor. Some people experience only one episode, though most have episodes throughout life. Some have a steady state of depressed mood, and untreated an episode can last years. Some experience psychotic symptoms with depression, more disabling, requires more intensive treatment.

Postpartum depression usually occurs in the first 30 days after delivery, ranges from postpartum blues to postpartum psychosis. Postpartum blues affects 50–80% of new mothers — anxiety, irritability, tearfulness. Postpartum psychosis affects fewer than 1% — over-concerned or delusional thinking about baby's health; often discovered at the 6-week checkup. Symptoms: tearful, flat affect, not wanting to hold the baby, feeling unable to care for the baby, wanting to give the baby away, fear the baby will be kidnapped, feeling like a bad mother. Important to screen moms prior to discharge for risk of postpartum depression.

Seasonal Affective Disorder — depression occurring in conjunction with seasonal change, most often fall/winter, remitting in spring. Higher latitude increases chances. Affects women more than men. Good intervention: light therapy.

MDD is a disabling disease: affects occupational and personal functioning, depressed mood every day causing misery/disability, causes difficulty in relationships, loss of ability to enjoy things once enjoyed, missed work/lost jobs/reduced effectiveness at work, may interfere with ADLs. High risk of suicide — if patient has a plan, 1:1 observation is necessary. Past behaviors predict future behaviors, so ask about past. Worst time for a suicidal person is in the morning. Nursing interventions: limit isolation, build self-esteem, therapeutic communication, trust, interject and give praise, patients need hope.

Stigma

Many people wrongly view depression as a personal weakness or character flaw, but depression — like all health problems — has a combination of biological and environmental causes. Contempt for mental illness is misguided and leads to stigma. Stigma is defined as a mark of shame or discredit. Personality, moods, and thoughts are made possible by biological brain activity shaped by environmental interactions (sum total of life experiences). Stigma extends even to some doctors, health administrators, and politicians, who may be less willing to support mental health programs/policies. Physicians may avoid entering the diagnosis to protect patients from stigma. Misunderstandings about depression lead some insurance companies to deny/lower coverage or deny payment for non-adherence. This leads to 80% of depression treatment occurring outside of mental health settings. Fear of stigma may delay/prevent treatment-seeking.

Symptoms of Depression (List)

Apathy, sadness, sleep disturbances, hopelessness, helplessness, worthlessness, guilt, anger; also fatigue, thoughts of death, decreased libido, psychomotor retardation, verbal self-bashing, talking about mass failure, spontaneous crying, dependency, passiveness.

Goals of Medical Treatment

Achieve complete remission and prevent recurrence. Ask patients how they solved problems in the past; make a "things to do" list; encourage exercise, journaling, calling friends/family; try not to be alone; make a pros/cons list of remaining on medication; let them know they can learn from mistakes.

Medical treatment involves medication, psychotherapy, and possibly ECT, light therapy. Most effective/gold standard = medication + CBT. Patients need to continue CBT — it's a must.

Psychotropic Medications — General

Effective for all types of depression; no single medication works for everyone so meds can be changed/titrated to reach therapeutic range. Allow 2–6 weeks for full effect. Specific drugs chosen because certain side effects are helpful. Most act on serotonin/norepinephrine; a few act on dopamine. Goal of antidepressants: alleviate depressive symptoms, restore normal mood, prevent recurrence, prevent a slide into mania in bipolar patients. Antidepressants can increase thoughts of suicide/behaviors in patients 24 years or younger.

SSRIs

Selectively block/inhibit reuptake of 5-HT (serotonin), causing larger synaptic concentrations of serotonin. Widely used as first-line drug of choice in the U.S. First drug of this kind: fluoxetine (Prozac). Others: citalopram, paroxetine, escitalopram, fluvoxamine, sertraline. Black box warning for risk of suicidal ideation (SI) and homicidal ideation (HI). Inhibit cytochrome P450 enzyme needed for specific drug metabolism — combining SSRIs with MAOIs can be fatal. Paroxetine (Paxil) is Category D and considered the only "safer" antidepressant option discussed for pregnant women in the lecture, and is also considered safe for older adults, though half-life is increased 2–3x in this population.

SNRIs

Includes duloxetine, venlafaxine, desvenlafaxine, and a newer SNRI, desvenlafaxine/Pristiq — spelled out with difficulty in the lecture ("that's a hard one to say"). A newer SNRI, levomilnacipran (Fetzima), causes less sexual side effects than previous drugs. Inhibit reuptake of both norepinephrine and serotonin; similar to tricyclics/SSRIs but do not significantly bind to muscarinic, histaminergic, or adrenergic receptors. Also slightly increase dopamine by blocking dopamine reuptake.

NDRIs

Bupropion (Wellbutrin, Zyban, Aplenzin). Only antidepressants that inhibit dopamine reuptake and do NOT affect serotonin. Should not be given with other dopamine reuptake inhibitors. Good alternative to SSRIs when sexual side effects are a problem. Also used for smoking cessation. Contraindicated in patients with seizures — lowers seizure threshold.

Novel Antidepressants

Mirtazapine (Remeron) — alpha-2 antagonist with 5-HT2 and 5-HT3 antagonism, used for major depression; fast onset, preferred over SSRIs for MDD in some cases; selectively blocks alpha-2 auto receptors to increase norepinephrine/serotonin via the presynaptic feedback system (not by inhibiting reuptake). Side effects similar to histamine effects — sedation, weight gain, increased cholesterol.

Vilazodone (Viibryd) — serotonin reuptake inhibitor + serotonin 1A agonist; less sexual side effects; blocks serotonin synthesis causing more to be made/released. Vortioxetine (Brintellix/Trintellix) — increases serotonin levels; good choice for cognitively impaired patients; long half-life up to 66 hours; highly protein-bound (check albumin labs).

TCAs (Tricyclics)

Metabolized primarily by cytochrome P450 enzymes 2D6, 1A2, 3A4. Often one of the drugs on standard drug screens due to overdose danger. Amitriptyline is highly anticholinergic. Desipramine is the most toxic tricyclic and should not be used in children. Oldest TCA: imipramine. Best TCA for older adults: nortriptyline (due to sedating effect useful for those depressed/agitated/with insomnia). TCAs increase CNS depression when combined with alcohol or benzodiazepines — educate patients not to drink alcohol or take benzos. Serious/fatal side effects can occur in combination with MAOIs. Reduced doses needed in older adults. Takes 2–4 weeks for therapeutic effect. Side effects: sedation, orthostatic hypotension, weight gain, dry mouth.

MAOIs

First antidepressants discovered but rarely used today due to severe adverse effects (fatal hypertensive crisis risk). Two named drugs: Nardil (phenelzine) and Parnate (tranylcypromine) — inhibit both variants of monoamine oxidase, increasing available biogenic amines in the brain. Hyperstimulation of CNS can occur, resulting in agitation, anxiety attacks, insomnia, restlessness. Fatal drug/food interactions can occur — education is critical. Teach patients not to eat tyramine-rich foods: aged/dairy products, alcohol, some fruits/vegetables including avocado/banana/fava beans, certain meats; avoid pizza and caffeinated drinks (as cited examples in lecture).

St. John's Wort

Over-the-counter herbal remedy with antidepressant action. Four capsules of St. John's Wort is equivalent to one dose of Prozac. Patients should NOT take St. John's Wort while taking Prozac or any other SSRI — the combination increases serotonin reuptake inhibition, leading to too much serotonin in the system, which can cause serotonin syndrome.

Serotonin Syndrome (Lecture Version)

A life-threatening drug reaction from high serotonin levels. Signs/symptoms: extreme restlessness (unable to sit), diaphoretic and shivering, complaints of headache and diarrhea. Common complaint: "I'm not feeling well" or feeling "different or funny." Always ask patients about all medications — prescribed, OTC, and herbal — when assessing.

Psychotherapy Types (Lecture)

Cognitive therapy — depression results from stressful situations being viewed as negative; reactions to stress grounded in childhood (loss of a parent through death/divorce/abandonment). Cognitive therapy aims at symptom removal by identifying/correcting negative thinking, replacing frameworks like "I can't do anything right" with "I can learn from my mistakes."

Behavioral therapy — uses CBT, psychotherapy, hypnotherapy, art therapy, dialectical behavior therapy to change feelings of helplessness/unworthiness into engagement with more positive, socially reinforcing attitudes.

Interpersonal psychotherapy — addresses role issues, social isolation, prolonged grief reactions, role transition, or major interpersonal themes; highly structured, time-limited (typically 12–16 weeks); focus is relieving symptoms and improving interpersonal functioning; patients should find a counselor they connect with.

ECT & TMS (Lecture)

ECT works with depressed patients when everything else fails. Patients must be NPO after midnight; check into the hospital the evening before. After the procedure, patient is sleepy with some mental confusion/memory loss.

TMS (transcranial magnetic stimulation) — FDA approved; magnetic impulses applied to the frontal cortex; electrical currents set at 0.2–0.8 causing a localized effect that opens new neuron pathways; patients need 6–12 treatments.

Suicide (Lecture)

Approximately 85 suicide deaths every day (as cited in lecture). Unfortunately most people who attempt suicide do not die, but some deaths are described as accidental. Increased risk for suicide among adolescents/young adults; firearm-related suicide accounts for almost 100% of this increase (as stated), related to firearms in the home. As a nurse, assess for suicidal ideation including gestures, threats, attempts. The nature of suicidal thinking: unbearable psychic pain, helplessness, hopelessness, loneliness, abandonment — "nobody cares." Risk factors for completed suicide: male, Caucasian or Native American. Suicide rates highest among older adults, white males highest risk — rate six times higher; person often saw their primary care doctor within three weeks prior to suicide.

When a patient describes symptoms of depression, ask directly: "Are you currently having any thoughts of harming yourself or others?" Assess for feelings of hopelessness and offer ways to find hope.

Depression in the Elderly (Lecture)

Older adults express different signs/symptoms of depression. Physical complaints such as frequent abdominal discomfort or headaches are common. Most older adults are UNLIKELY to tell their doctor they are depressed. Signs/symptoms can be complicated by cognitive impairment or present as dementia, leading to missed/underdiagnosis. Symptoms may be viewed by providers as a normal response to aging. Older adults have a 1.5–3x greater risk of death if depressed — reasons can include stopping eating or refusing treatment (e.g., dialysis) because they understand the outcome.

Bipolar Disorder (Lecture)

Sometimes referred to as manic depressive or bipolar affective disorder. Patients most often experience both mania and depression, though a depressive episode is not required for a Bipolar I diagnosis (one manic episode is sufficient). Mania characterized by constant/indiscriminate enthusiasm, fluctuating between elation and irritability; activity seen as productive to the patient but is actually disorganized/unproductive to others; grandiose delusions and decreased need for sleep. Manic patients may hit on/get in the face of the nurse, believe they're the most important person on the unit, want to tell you all about themselves, may check back into the hospital just to see a particular staff member. Nursing intervention: back away, establish the nurse-client relationship, let them know they matter, exhibit professional boundaries.

Symptoms during mania: elevated mood, grandiosity, inflated self-esteem, anger, flight of ideas, racing thoughts. Symptoms during depression: withdrawal, passivity, fatigue, anorexia, hypersexuality (noted as occurring in manic phase context in lecture).

In a manic phase, patients act differently on the unit — during group sessions they may dominate conversation and not let others interject; ask them to leave the group if this happens (to their room or occupational therapy); they may get upset and tell staff off but will typically forget quickly. For a patient feeling powerless, encourage daily group therapy attendance.

Manic patients have rapid/pressured speech, are easily distractible, are multitaskers with poor outcomes, show unwarranted optimism/grandiosity/poor judgment. Mania impairs ability to function and threatens lives. In a manic state, patients may wear excessive/bright clothing or no clothes at all, have grandiose thoughts/ideas, spend a lot of money with poor impulse control/judgment (can spend an entire paycheck at a casino or on large purchases); females may wear excessive makeup/jewelry; patients may be admitted with large amounts of cash on hand (example cited: up to $10,000). When a person goes for extended periods without sleep during mania, hallucinations can start due to lack of sleep.

Bipolar disorder recurs in families due to a genetic component — a multigene inheritance disorder. Average age of onset is early 20s. Children may show behavior problems before their first episode. Episodes begin suddenly; psychosocial factors can influence the timing of episodes. Without treatment, episodes average about 4 per year. Episodes can last a few weeks to several months. If accompanied by psychosis, more serious and more likely to lead to aggression or suicide; can also be accompanied by substance abuse.

Priorities for bipolar patients: always safety and treating the mood disorder. Comprehensive approach includes education, psychotherapy, and active involvement of the client in treatment/recovery.

Psychotropic Agents for Bipolar (Lecture)

Lithium — compliance is an important issue as side effects may be intolerable; client education essential. Side effects: nausea, dry mouth, diarrhea, thirst, drowsiness, mild hand tremors, polyuria, weight gain, bloated feeling, sleeplessness, lightheadedness. Polyuria/polydipsia occur in ~60% of patients on lithium; side effects decrease after about 3–6 weeks of use. Lithium is generally contraindicated in cardiovascular disease and can negatively affect the kidneys, so contraindicated in renal disease — check creatinine levels. Can cause nephrogenic diabetes insipidus in a significant number of patients. Another side effect: neuropathy. Client education: monitor fluid/salt intake, assess for lithium toxicity. About 70–80% of individuals who take lithium long-term for prophylaxis, and about 50% treated for the acute phase, have a therapeutic response.

Valproate — first-line drug used alone or in combination with lithium. Valproate/Depakote approved for treatment of mania as of 1995. Effectiveness may relate to inhibition of kindling activity in the brain; works to increase the inhibitory role of GABA, suppress sodium influx into the neuron, and suppress calcium influx through specific calcium channels. Advantages: rapid onset; disadvantages: transient hair loss, weight gain, tremors, GI upset, menstrual disturbances in women, reduction in intelligence in children exposed in utero (major concern). Bioavailability ~80%; high protein binding (check albumin levels); half-life 5–15 hours but reaches steady state in 2–5 days.

Carbamazepine (Tegretol) — used in clients who don't respond to lithium or other drugs; often used with rapidly cycling bipolar episodes. Typically well tolerated but has side effects of nausea, anorexia, sometimes vomiting, sedation, drowsiness. Most serious adverse effect: agranulocytosis — obtain a weekly CBC when the patient starts this drug. Drugs that inhibit cytochrome P450 3A4 enzymes (such as SSRIs) can cause adverse effects — avoid mixing.

Lamictal (lamotrigine) — approved to treat bipolar including bipolar depression; manipulates the GABA system and inhibits neuronal firing. Numerous side effects; can cause a moderate skin rash and potentially fatal Stevens-Johnson syndrome in 1–2% of children — described in lecture as the skin becoming necrotic and falling off, similar in appearance to a burn victim; reversible once the patient stops the medication. Also mentioned: trileptal (oxcarbazepine) and rotigotine/other agents used in treating bipolar disorder.

Nursing Care/Assessment (Lecture)

Mental Status Assessment includes: self-assessment, appearance and effect, psychomotor activity, thought processes — should be done at least once every 24 hours, sometimes each shift depending on facility. Assess response to drug therapy (is it therapeutic?). Screen routinely for depression. Screen depressed persons for suicide risk — if suicidal ideation present, institute 1:1 observation; do not leave the patient; place close to the nurses' station if necessary; keep the environment safe. Ask the patient for a "contract" (verbal agreement not to act on suicidal ideation) — many patients will honor an agreement made with the nurse.

Documentation: accurately document appearance (disheveled, unkempt, body odor = signs of worsening depression), time spent in bed (e.g., "in bed 8 of 10 hours on shift, up only for bathroom"), refusal of meals/ADLs/speech — frame objectively/behaviorally rather than subjectively.

Nursing diagnoses: risk for violence, impaired social interaction, imbalanced nutrition. Implementation/interventions aimed at improving/reducing: risk for violence (self-directed = SI, directed at others = HI), impaired social interactions, hopelessness, disturbed thought process.

Evaluation: determine if desired outcomes were met; if not, revise plan of care and provide feedback. Discharge considerations: client education, ongoing psychotherapy, support groups, emergency numbers. Advocate for clients by being active in the community — described as one of the biggest roles nurses can play, since nurses understand the illness and can be a voice for positive change/outcomes for mentally ill patients.

Lecture Transcript — Schizophrenia

Schizophrenia — complex disorder of the brain, occurs in 1 out of 100 people, alters perception, thought, feelings, and behaviors. Patients often experience considerable distress in personal and work lives, are likely to suffer prejudice/discrimination, are underrepresented in research funding/services — a major public health issue.

Class discussion note: increase in dopamine implicated; cannabis use noted as a risk factor for worsening/triggering symptoms.

Symptoms (Lecture)

Positive/psychosis symptoms: hallucinations, delusions, disorganized thinking — "I believe I can serve... I communicate with the satellites." Negative symptoms: flat, don't show emotion, lack of motivation to do things (showering, cleaning up), social isolation, neglect of ADLs. Disorganized symptoms: disorganized thoughts, incongruency between emotions and content of what they're telling you (example given: a patient laughing while describing their dog's death).

Discussion point: the issue with many psychotropics that treat positive symptoms is that they can decrease dopamine too low, causing negative symptoms to increase — the treatment goal is to balance this. Mixing an antipsychotic with an antidepressant can be appropriate if a patient has co-occurring depression or bipolar disorder — when schizophrenia occurs with a mood disorder, this is called schizoaffective disorder.

Subtypes (Lecture — historical, DSM-5 removed formal subtyping)

Disorganized subtype — lack of catatonia with disorganized speech/behavior, inappropriate expressions. Catatonic subtype — waxy flexibility, patient stays fixed in a position, may stare; agitated catatonia includes strange repetitive movements, smacking of lips, wringing of hands. Echolalia — repeating what another says, "echo + repetition."

Paranoid schizophrenia — patients often have auditory hallucinations; may believe the military/government is watching or poisoning them (a personal example was shared regarding a family member with paranoid schizophrenia and military-related delusions). Note: even once stabilized on medication, the underlying theme of a delusion may remain even if hallucination frequency decreases — the person may still hear voices but describe them differently (e.g., telling jokes) once medicated, though they no longer act on them the same way as when unmedicated. Undifferentiated means the patient fits more than one subtype.

DSM-5 removed subtyping in favor of a spectrum model (mild-moderate-severe), similar to other mental health diagnoses. Treatment does not change based on subtype. This is included in the course because some facilities (e.g., state hospitals like Patton) still use and document these subtype terms.

ECT and Neuroplasticity (Lecture)

ECT is theorized to generate more neurons and grow dendrites, potentially allowing relearning with continued medication compliance. A cited example: a state hospital opened a unit specifically for the 10 most violent mentally disabled patients who all undergo ECT treatment due to a high success rate.

Gender-Specific Patterns (Lecture)

Mental disorders often affect genders differently: schizophrenia affects men more than women; borderline personality disorder affects women more than men; social anxiety disorder affects men more than women (as stated in lecture) — relevant for exam scenario questions (consider patient gender).

Discussion: many historic psychiatric theorists (Maslow, Freud) developed frameworks in the 1800s — the class is encouraged to think critically about how understanding has evolved since then and what has changed.

Culture and Environment (Lecture)

Consider where the patient is from — different cultures view mental health differently (e.g., Native American use of peyote and different framing of hallucinations/visions as spiritual). Consider religious beliefs and socioeconomic status — financial background, upbringing location. Example given: patients from areas with high homelessness and fewer resources.

Genetic predisposition does not guarantee schizophrenia will develop — environmental factors and stress often "bring out" the disease. With identical twins, if one twin develops schizophrenia, the concordance rate for the other twin is about 55%. If a child with genetic predisposition is placed in a stable, supportive home, they may never develop schizophrenia; in an unstable/abusive home, outcomes could differ — though resilience is also a protective factor.

Only 60% of patients with schizophrenia actually receive treatment — true prevalence may be higher than statistics reflect since data mostly comes from hospital settings. Behaviors before diagnosis can include petty theft (example: a patient threw a rock through a neighbor's window believing evil spirits needed to be let out) up to more severe acts including rape and murder in rare cases. Patients with schizophrenia are often homeless, can be victims of crime, and are easily manipulated due to their delusions.

Estrogen is theorized to be a protective hormone against the development of schizophrenia, which may explain lower prevalence in women.

Disorganized Thinking & Behavior (Lecture)

Disorganized thinking includes incoherent speech, loose associations, possibly word salad. Disorganized behaviors include not fulfilling ADLs (mostly due to lack of motivation), inappropriate dress (example: bright colored jewelry cited), inappropriate personal behavior affecting relationships leading to further isolation; some patients develop catatonia along with disorganized behaviors.

Negative Symptom Definitions (Lecture Review)

Alogia, avolition, and anhedonia were emphasized as terms to know: Alogia relates to poverty/lack of speech, Avolition relates to lack of motivation, Anhedonia relates to lack of joy/pleasure in activities.

Course of Disease (Lecture)

Disease often develops between ages 14–24. For women, onset can be later, sometimes into their 30s. Symptoms peak after about a decade, then plateau rather than continuously worsening. Patients with schizophrenia can develop dementia at younger ages than the general population at a higher rate. Relapses can occur even after years of medication stability, often requiring medication changes. When a patient stops medication after discharge, symptoms return and they may be re-hospitalized — the medication that worked before may not work again because the neurons in the brain have changed, making treatment "tricky."

Class discussion point: onset of psychosis could be related to major life stressors such as moving out or relationship breakups, in addition to genetic and neurodevelopmental factors.

Kaplan Content Review — Section 4: Depressive Disorders

Depressive Disorders — an overwhelming state of sadness, loss of interest or pleasure, feelings of guilt, disturbed sleep and appetite, low energy, and an inability to concentrate. Can be manifested as a single episode or recurrent pattern; varies according to age, race, gender; classified as a mood disorder.

Types

  • Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) — types include melancholia, postpartum, psychotic, agitated, seasonal affective disorder (SAD); characterized by symptoms persisting over a minimum 2-week period; involves psychological, biological, and social factors.
  • Dysthymic Disorder
  • Depressive Disorder, Not Otherwise Specified (NOS)
  • Gerontologic considerations — high risk of suicide; depression underdiagnosed and undertreated; contributing factors include illness and decreased physical/mental function.

Assessment

Possible changes in self-esteem/self-confidence: low self-esteem, self-deprecation, feelings of helplessness/hopelessness, obsessive thoughts and fears, rumination and worries, sense of doom/failure, regressed behavior (immature, demanding).

Possible changes in self-care: unkempt/depressed appearance, multiple physical complaints, prone to injury/accidents/infections, lack of energy/fatigue, changes in usual sleep pattern (insomnia or hypersomnia, feels unrested after night's sleep), weight loss/poor appetite or weight gain, constipation, amenorrhea, lack of sexual desire.

Possible changes in cognitive/mental functioning: decreased attention span, decreased concentration, slowed speech and thought processes, slowed motor activity, impaired reality testing (psychotic depression), withdrawn, ambivalent and indecisive behavior, agitation and psychomotor restlessness, suicidal ideation.

Characteristics of Depression (Bipolar)

  1. Weight gain or loss, anorexia
  2. Insomnia or hypersomnia
  3. Decreased sexual desire
  4. Fatigue, decreased activity
  5. May feel hopeless, unhappy, helpless, miserable, guilty
  6. Regressive thinking — passive, dependent, decreased motivation, concerned with bodily functions, impaired memory, distorted thought content, slow and diminished speech
  7. Delusions possible
  8. Socially withdrawn
  9. Poor self-esteem

Etiology

Psychodynamics: Bipolar disorder is an affective disorder; reality contact is less disturbed than in schizophrenia; elation or grandiosity can be a defense against underlying depression or feelings of low self-esteem; testing/manipulative behavior results from poor self-esteem; hypomania is a less extreme form.

Predisposing factors: Hereditary/genetic; biochemical; involvement with alcohol/drugs common (self-medication).

Problems: Easily stimulated by surroundings (hyperactive and anxious, unable to meet physical needs); disruptive and intrusive; denial; testing, manipulative, demanding behavior; superficial social relationships.

Nursing Management — Hyperactivity/Agitation

Simplify the environment and decrease stimuli: assign to a single room away from activity, keep noise level low, soft lighting. Limit people; anticipate overstimulating situations/activities and remove client to quiet areas. Distract and redirect energy: choose activities for brief attention span (chores, walks); choose physical activities using large movements until acute mania subsides (e.g., dance); provide writing materials for busy work once acute mania subsides (political suggestions, plans).

Provide External Controls

Assign one staff person to provide controls; do not encourage client when telling jokes or performing (avoid laughing); accompany client to room when hyperactivity is escalating; guard vigilantly against suicide as elation subsides and mood evens out.

Manage Manipulativeness

Set limits (e.g., limit phone calls when excessive); set firm consistent times for meetings — client often late and unaware of time; refuse unreasonable demands; explain restrictions on behavior and reasons so client does not feel rejected. Communicate using a firm, unambivalent, consistent approach: use staff consistency in enforcing rules; remain nonjudgmental (e.g., when client disrobes say "I will not allow you to undress here"); never threaten or make comparisons with others, as it increases hostility and poor coping. Avoid long, complicated discussions: use short sentences with specific straightforward responses; avoid giving advice when solicited (e.g., "I notice you want me to take responsibility for your life.")

Meet Physical Needs

Encourage fluids; offer water every hour because client will not take the time to drink. Give high-calorie finger foods and drinks to be carried while moving (cupcakes, sandwiches). Serve meals on tray in client's room when too stimulated. Encourage rest: sedate PRN; encourage short naps. Supervise bathing routines when client plays with water or is too distracted to clean self.

Decrease Denial and Increase Awareness

Encourage expression of real feelings through reflecting. Help client acknowledge the need for help when denying it (e.g., "You say you don't need love, but most people need love. It's okay to feel that."). Function as a role model for client by communicating feelings openly. Help client recognize demanding behavior (e.g., "You seem to want others to notice you."). Encourage client to recognize needs of others. Have client verbalize needs directly (e.g., wishes for attention).

Kaplan Content Review — Section 6: Altered Thought Processes (Schizophrenia)

Manifestations of Altered Thought Processes — Characteristic Findings

  1. Withdrawal from relationships and from the world — neologisms/rhyming, magical thinking, social ineptitude (aloof, fails to encourage interpersonal relationships)
  2. Inappropriate or no display of feelings
  3. Hypochondriasis, depersonalization
  4. Suspiciousness — sees world as a hostile, threatening place
  5. Poor reality testing — hallucinations; delusions (persistent false beliefs): Grandeur (belief one is special, e.g., a monarch), Persecutory (belief one is a victim of a plot), Ideas of reference (belief environmental events are directed toward the self); Illusions — misperceptions of reality
  6. Loose associations
  7. Short attention span, decreased ability to comprehend stimuli
  8. Psychomotor alteration — slow-moving, slow-speaking
  9. Hyperactivity — loud, rapid talking; inability to sit still (akathisia)
  10. Regression
  11. Inability to meet basic survival needs — unable to feed self properly (poor nutritional habits), poor personal hygiene, inappropriate dress for the weather/environment

Definitions

  • Hallucinations — perceptual dysfunction in which false sensory perceptions are experienced in the absence of an external stimulus; visual and auditory hallucinations most common.
  • Delusions — persistent false beliefs, rigidly held, which do not stand the test of reality. Common types: Delusions of grandeur (that one is special in a way with no basis in reality, e.g., President of the United States); Persecutory (that one is being threatened, e.g., a victim of a plot); Ideas of reference (that situations/events involve them, e.g., thinks people are talking about client; misconstruing trivial remarks); Somatic (that one's body is reacting in a particular way without a basis in reality).
  • Illusion — misperception of reality.
  • Neologisms — privately invented words that people do not understand.
  • Magical thinking — primitive thought process in which one believes thoughts alone can change events.
  • Looseness of associations — incoherent, illogical flow of thoughts and ideas producing confusing language.
  • Autistic thinking — regressive thought process in which subjective, personal interpretations are not validated with objective reality.
  • Word salad — words combined with no logical sequence.

Conditions Predisposing to Withdrawal from Reality

  • Senile dementia
  • Acute drug psychosis
  • Biochemical interaction
  • Ineffective family interaction
  • Schizophrenia

Sub-Types of Schizophrenia — Full Definitions

Disorganized — type of schizophrenia characterized by inappropriate or flat affect; social withdrawal extreme; disorganized speech and behavior; silliness, inappropriate laughter, grimacing, and regression common.

Catatonic — stuporous condition associated with rigidity, posturing, waxy flexibility (when limb positioned, stays in that position); may alternate with overactivity and agitation; insidious onset.

Paranoid — mainly features suspiciousness, distortion, and projection; delusions of grandeur, persecution, or hallucinations may be prominent; if onset occurs later in life, client tends to be less withdrawn from daily activities.

Undifferentiated — characterized by general symptoms of schizophrenia but does not meet criteria for a particular type (poor overall functioning).

Residual — history of at least one psychotic episode, but currently without overt psychotic behavior; may be withdrawn but is able to function.

Nursing Management — Altered Thought Processes

Maintain client safety — protect from altered thought processes: decrease sensory stimuli; remove from areas of tension; validate reality; recognize that client is experiencing hallucination; do not argue with client; respond to feeling or tone of hallucination or delusion; do not reinforce hallucinations; be alert to hallucinations that command the client to harm self or others or to do destructive acts.

Protect from erratic and inappropriate behavior: communicate in calm, authoritative tone; address client by name; observe client for early signs of escalating behavior. Administer antipsychotic medications as indicated.

Establish a therapeutic relationship — engage in individual therapy: institute measures to promote trust; be consistent and reliable in keeping all scheduled appointments; avoid direct questions (client may feel threatened); accept client's indifference (e.g., failure to smile or greet nurse) and avoidance behavior (e.g., hostility or sarcasm); explain staff changes, especially vacations and absences; encourage client's affect by verbalizing what you observe (e.g., "You seem to think that I don't want to stay."); tolerate silences — may have to sit through long silences with client who is too withdrawn to speak (catatonic); accept regression as a normal part of treatment when new stresses are encountered. With delusional regression, respond to associated feeling, not to the delusion (e.g., client claims he has no heart — nursing response: "You must feel empty."); help pinpoint source of regression (e.g., anxiety about discharge).

Meet physical needs of severely regressed clients (e.g., catatonic): poor basic hygiene — may have to be washed initially; poor nutritional habits — may have to be fed (ask client to pick up fork; if unable, then feeding is necessary; when ready, encourage client to eat in dining room with others).

Engage in family therapy — especially when client is returning to family: understand the problem involves the family — establish a "family client" in need of support; family helps maintain pathology; client is often the scapegoat for family problems; all members must be involved to effect change in client; decrease fusion — give all members a sense of self and independence.

Engage in socialization or activity group therapy according to client's ability: accept nonverbal behavior initially; if client cannot tolerate group, do not force or embarrass; act as a social role model for client. Provide simple activities or tasks to promote positive self-esteem and success — finger painting and clay are good choices for regressed catatonic client; encourage attendance at occupational, vocational, and art therapy. Avoid competitive situations with paranoid client; solitary activities are better.
